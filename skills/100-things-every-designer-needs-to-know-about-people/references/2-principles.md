# Key Principles: Readability & Visual Communication

> Source: "100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People" by Susan M. Weinschenk, Ph.D. (Second Edition, 2020) — Chapters 13-18 (How People Read), 9-12 (Color & Visual Grouping)

## 1. Reading is Anticipation, Not Absorption

People don't read letter by letter — they anticipate upcoming words using pattern recognition. This has profound implications for text design.

### The Mechanics of Reading

| Component | What Happens | Duration |
|---|---|---|
| **Saccade** | Quick eye jump forward (7-9 letters) | ~20-30ms (effectively blind during) |
| **Fixation** | Still moment where reading happens | ~250ms |
| **Regression** | Eye jumps backward 10-15% of the time | Rereading letters/words |
| **Perceptual Span** | Reading ahead (~15 letters) | Peripheral vision used |

> **Case: The Scrambled Paragraph** (Ch. 14): "Eevn touhgh the wrosd are srcmaelbd, cahnecs are taht you can raed tihs..." Readers can read scrambled text as long as first and last letters are correct. **Key takeaway**: Reading is about anticipation, not letter-by-letter decoding.

### Key Readability Principles

| Principle | Rule | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **All caps = shouting** | Not inherently harder to read, but unfamiliar and interpreted as shouting | Ch. 13 |
| **Serif vs. Sans serif** | No difference in readability — choose for brand/mood | Ch. 15 |
| **Hard-to-read font = hard task** | Users transfer font difficulty to task difficulty estimation | Ch. 15 (Song & Schwarz, 2008) |
| **X-height matters** | Fonts with larger x-heights look bigger at same point size | Ch. 16 |
| **Screen < Paper** | Screen reading is harder — use larger fonts, higher contrast, shorter paragraphs | Ch. 17 |
| **Line length tradeoff** | 100 chars/line = fastest reading. 45-72 chars/line = preferred | Ch. 18 |

> **Case: Exercise Instructions Study** (Ch. 15): When exercise instructions were in Arial, people estimated 8 minutes and rated the exercise as easy. When the same text was in Brush Script MT Italic, people estimated 15 minutes and rated it as difficult. **Key takeaway**: If text is hard to read, the task itself feels harder.

## 2. Color Principles: Perception, Culture & Accessibility

### Chromostereopsis (Ch. 10)
Red and blue together (or red and green) cause depth illusion — one color appears to float above the other. This is visually tiring and should be avoided.

**Rule**: Never put red/blue or red/green adjacent in high-contrast contexts (text on background, thin lines).

### Color Blindness (Ch. 11)
- 9% of men, 0.5% of women have red-green color deficiency
- Most common: difficulty distinguishing red/yellow/green
- Use redundant coding: color + shape, color + texture, color + position

> **Case: The CDC Flu Map** (Ch. 11): The CDC website uses a color palette that looks identical to people with red-green, blue-yellow, and full color vision — varying shades of brown and yellow. **Key takeaway**: Universal color schemes exist. Use them.

### Cultural Color Meanings (Ch. 12)
| Color | U.S. Meaning | Other Cultures |
|---|---|---|
| White | Weddings, purity | Death, mourning (many Asian cultures) |
| Red | Danger, stop, financial loss | Happiness (China), celebration (India) |
| Green | Go, money, environment | Luck (Ireland), sacred (Islam) |
| Gold | Success, high quality | Similar worldwide |

> **Case: The Red Revenue Map** (Ch. 12): A VP of Sales presented a color-coded revenue map where the Western region was red. The financial staff audibly gasped — to accountants, red means losing money. **Key takeaway**: Color meanings are context-dependent. Know your audience.

### The Proximity Principle (Ch. 9)
Items close together are perceived as belonging together. The left-to-right connection is stronger than top-to-bottom. Before using borders or boxes, try spacing adjustments.

## 3. Affordance Design Principles

Based on Chapters 7-8:

1. **Perceived affordances** (Norman's concept): Objects must communicate how to interact with them — buttons should look pressable, links should look clickable
2. **Shadows create depth**: Button shadows signal "pushability." Flat design reduces affordance cues
3. **Hyperlinks are losing cues**: Blue underlined text is disappearing; hover-only cues require discovery
4. **Change blindness (Ch. 8)**: People miss visual changes they don't expect — use animation or sound for critical updates

### Takeaways for Design
- Don't assume users will notice changes, even obvious ones
- Use multiple cues (color + text + icon + position) for critical information
- Test affordances with new users — what looks clickable to you may not be
