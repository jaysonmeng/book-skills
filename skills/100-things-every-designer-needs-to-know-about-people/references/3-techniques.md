## The Design Psychology Toolbox

### The 4-Item Rule in Practice
- **Navigation:** 4 primary nav items max. If you need more, chunk into categories of 4.
- **Forms:** 4 fields per step. Group related fields into 4-item chunks.
- **Features:** Pitch 4 key features. Not 7. Not 10. Four.
- **Pricing:** 3-4 tiers. The middle one is the "decoy" that makes the top one look reasonable.

### The 10-Minute Window Design Pattern
- Every screen/feature should allow a user to arrive, complete a task, and leave within 10 minutes.
- If a task takes longer, insert break points every 10 minutes.
- Use progress indicators to show completion stage (counteracts the "when will this end?" anxiety).
- After 10 minutes, feature a "natural break" — a visual or interactive shift.

### Recognition Over Recall Checklist
- [ ] Can users see available options instead of remembering them?
- [ ] Are icons and labels consistent with mental models?
- [ ] Does the search work better than the navigation?
- [ ] Are recently used items surfaced automatically?
- [ ] Does the system remember user preferences?

### Variable Reward Design (Ethical)
Apply variable rewards to engagement features:
- Different content each visit (not the same feed)
- Occasional "surprise" elements (bonus, discovery, delight)
- Intermittent reinforcement for desired behaviors
- Never use variable rewards for notification spam — this is unethical

> **Case: The dopamine loop of checking email** (Thing 52): Every time you check your email and find something new, dopamine is released. But the release happens during ANTICIPATION of the check, not from finding the email itself. This is why people compulsively check — the anticipation is the drug. **Key takeaway:** Design anticipation into your product. The moment before discovery is more powerful than the discovery itself.

### Error Recovery Design
- **Undo:** Always provide a way to undo any action. This reduces user anxiety by 10x.
- **Clear feedback:** Tell users what happened and what to do next. Not "Error 42" but "Your internet connection was lost. Check your Wi-Fi settings and try again."
- **Forgiveness:** Don't punish users for mistakes. If they delete something important, don't make them contact support — give them a trash bin.
- **Stress testing:** Test your interface when users are under time pressure, tired, or distracted. This is when real errors happen.

### Color Accessibility Checklist
- Don't use color alone to convey information
- Red + blue text/borders are hard on the eyes (chromatic aberration)
- Test all designs in grayscale
- 9% of male users are color-blind — use patterns and labels
- High contrast text improves readability for everyone

### Social Design Principles
- Online interactions follow social rules (be polite and personal)
- People mirror each other's language and behavior
- Laughter bonds groups together (add humor naturally)
- Group size limit for strong ties is ~150 (Dunbar's number)
- Use real human faces — stock photos reduce trust

### The Choice Design Framework
1. Default to the desired action (people are lazy)
2. Limit options to 4 (max)
3. Show the most popular choice (social proof)
4. Make sure the first option is the one you want (primacy effect)
5. Use 3-tier pricing with a decoy (asymmetrical dominance)

> **Case: The one-button camera** (Thing 58): When designing a camera for elderly users, the team wanted to include 20+ features. Instead, they created a single-button interface: point and shoot. Everything else was automatic or hidden in a menu. Usage increased 10x over the feature-rich competitor. **Key takeaway:** People are inherently lazy. The simplest path will be the most used. The best design often means removing features, not adding them.

> **Case: The progress bar experiment** (Thing 50): A coffee shop loyalty card experiment showed 10 stamps. One group had 0 of 10 filled at start. Another had 2 of 10 pre-filled (progress toward goal). The pre-filled group completed their cards significantly faster and returned more often. **Key takeaway:** Show progress toward a goal. Artificial progress (pre-filled stamps) increases motivation. The closer people get, the more motivated they become.
