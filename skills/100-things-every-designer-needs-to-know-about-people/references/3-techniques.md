# Techniques: Attention, Motivation & Habit Design

> Source: "100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People" by Susan M. Weinschenk, Ph.D. (Second Edition, 2020) — Chapters 40-49 (How People Focus Their Attention), 50-62 (What Motivates People), 27-28 (Thinking & Loads)

## 1. Attention Techniques

### The 10-Minute Attention Limit (Ch. 44)
Sustained attention lasts about 10 minutes. After that, people need a break, a change, or a compelling reason to continue.

**Design application**: Break content into 10-minute chunks. Introduce new elements, questions, or interactions at ~10-minute intervals.

### What Grabs Attention (Ch. 47)
Ranked by effectiveness:
1. **Danger** — threat signals (evolutionary priority)
2. **Food** — biological need
3. **Sex** — reproductive imperative
4. **Movement** — peripheral vision trigger (also the most annoying)
5. **Faces** — FFA activation (Ch. 4)
6. **Stories** — narrative processing (Ch. 33)

> **Case: Peripheral Annoyance** (Ch. 2): Blinking/flashing ads on screen peripheries grab attention because peripheral vision detects movement in ~80ms (vs. 140-190ms for central vision). **Key takeaway**: Don't put distracting animation in peripheral vision if you want users to concentrate.

### The Multitasking Myth (Ch. 46)
People are worse at multitasking than they think. What feels like multitasking is actually rapid task-switching, which degrades performance on both tasks.

### Habituation (Ch. 41)
People stop noticing repeated information. The same ad, notification, or UI pattern loses effectiveness over time. Vary presentation to maintain attention.

### Salient Cues (Ch. 45)
One different element (color, shape, orientation) grabs attention. Multiple differences cancel each other out — nothing stands out when everything is different.

## 2. Motivation Techniques

### The Goal-Gradient Effect (Ch. 50)
People accelerate motivation as they get closer to a goal. A 10% progress bar generates more motivation than a 0% bar — even though the effort required is the same.

**Design application**: Always show progress. Coffee shop loyalty cards that come pre-stamped with 2 of 10 stamps increase completion rates more than starting from 0.

### Variable Rewards (Ch. 51-53)
- **Dopamine** drives information-seeking behavior (Ch. 52)
- **Variable rewards** (random, unpredictable) are more motivating than fixed rewards
- **Unpredictability** keeps people searching and returning

> **Case: Dopamine Study** (Ch. 52): Research shows dopamine is released not when the reward arrives, but during the anticipation. The uncertainty of whether a reward will come is more motivating than the reward itself. **Key takeaway**: Variable schedules (like slot machines or pull-to-refresh) drive engagement because uncertainty fuels dopamine.

### Intrinsic > Extrinsic (Ch. 54)
People are more motivated by internal drives (mastery, purpose, progress) than external rewards (money, points, badges). External rewards can actually undermine intrinsic motivation.

### Autonomy, Mastery, Control (Ch. 55, 62)
- **Autonomy**: People need to feel in control of their actions
- **Mastery**: People are motivated by getting better at something
- **Control**: Perceived control reduces stress and increases engagement

> **Case: The Shortcut Study** (Ch. 58): People will only look for shortcuts if the shortcuts themselves are easy to find. If finding a shortcut requires effort, people will stick with the harder path. **Key takeaway**: Make the right action the easiest action.

### Social Motivation (Ch. 56)
Social norms motivate more than economic incentives. People cooperate, share, and contribute because of social expectations, not just personal benefit.

### Competition When Fewer Competitors (Ch. 61)
People are more motivated to compete when there are fewer competitors. A top-10 list is more motivating than a top-100 list. Being #3 of 20 feels more achievable than #300 of 3,000.

## 3. Progressive Disclosure (Ch. 27)

The single most important technique for managing information density:

1. Show only what users need at this moment
2. Provide clear paths to deeper information (links, expand/collapse, accordion)
3. Count understanding, not clicks — people don't mind clicking if each click gives them what they expect

> **Case: Social Security Website** (Ch. 27): The SSA survivor benefits page scrolls through 7 full screens of undifferentiated content. With progressive disclosure, each topic would be summarized in 1-2 sentences with click-to-expand. **Key takeaway**: Long undifferentiated pages overwhelm. Break into layers.

### The ARCS Model (Keller, Ch. 27)
Progressive disclosure originates from instructional design:
- **Attention**: Grab interest
- **Relevance**: Connect to user's needs
- **Confidence**: Show achievability
- **Satisfaction**: Reward completion

## 4. Habit Formation (Ch. 60)

Forming or changing a habit is easier than people think, but requires:
1. **Consistent context** — same trigger, same time, same place
2. **Low friction** — the easiest action is the one taken
3. **Immediate reward** — even small positive feedback reinforces the loop
4. **Variable schedule** — unpredictable rewards are more habit-forming than fixed ones

### Design Patterns for Habits
- **Implementation intentions**: "When X happens, I will do Y" — link habit to existing trigger
- **Stacking**: New habit after established habit (e.g., "After I brush my teeth, I will floss one tooth")
