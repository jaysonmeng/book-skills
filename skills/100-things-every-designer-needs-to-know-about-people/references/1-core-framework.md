## The 10 Categories of Human Psychology for Design

Susan Weinschenk organizes her 100 design principles into 10 categories. Each category answers a fundamental question about how people interact with designed experiences.

### 1. How People See (Things 1-12)
The visual system is not a camera — it's a construction. The brain fills in, guesses, and filters.
- **Key principles:** Peripheral vision gets the gist; central vision focuses on details. People scan in F-patterns based on past experience. Faces are processed by a dedicated brain region. Gestalt principles (proximity groups things). 9% of men are color-blind. Red and blue together cause visual stress.
- **Design implication:** Don't rely on color alone. Place key info in the center. Use Gestalt grouping.

> **Case: The F-pattern discovery** (Thing 6): Eye-tracking studies show that people scan screens in a pattern that looks like the letter F — two horizontal strips at the top, then a vertical strip down the left side. This is driven by expectation (from years of reading text). **Key takeaway:** Put the most important information in the top-left area. Users won't read the whole page — they'll scan the F-pattern.

### 2. How People Read (Things 13-18)
Reading on screen is harder than reading on paper — 25% slower. People don't read word by word; they recognize patterns.
- **Key principles:** All-caps is NOT harder to read than lowercase (myth busted!). Longer lines are read faster but shorter lines are preferred. Comprehension ≠ reading speed. Font size matters more than font choice.
- **Design implication:** Use larger fonts for screens. Don't assume all-caps hurts readability.

### 3. How People Remember (Things 19-26)
Short-term memory holds only 4 items (not 7±2). Memories are reconstructed each time, not played back.
- **Key principles:** Recognition is much easier than recall. Use information or lose it. Memory takes mental resources. The most vivid memories are often wrong.
- **Design implication:** Never make users remember information from one screen to another. Show, don't ask.

### 4. How People Think (Things 27-39)
People process in chunks, create mental models, and are driven by stories.
- **Key principles:** Mental models shape expectations. People learn best from examples and stories. Minds wander 30% of the time. People screen out information that doesn't fit their beliefs.
- **Design implication:** Match existing mental models. Use stories, not data dumps. Chunk into bite-sized pieces.

> **Case: The F-pattern carryover to digital** (Thing 27): When users encounter a new app, they carry mental models from every app they've used before. If your navigation behaves differently from every other app, users will struggle. **Key takeaway:** Innovate on function, not on familiar patterns. If you must break a pattern, teach the new one explicitly.

### 5. How People Focus Their Attention (Things 40-49)
Attention is selective and limited. Sustained attention lasts about 10 minutes. Multitasking is a myth.
- **Key principles:** Attention habituates (repeated stimuli become invisible). Well-practiced skills don't require conscious attention. Danger, food, sex, movement, faces, and stories get priority attention.
- **Design implication:** Design for 10-minute blocks. Use motion and faces to capture attention. Don't design for multitasking — design for focus.

### 6. What Motivates People (Things 50-62)
Variable rewards are powerful. Dopamine drives seeking behavior. Intrinsic > extrinsic.
- **Key principles:** People are more motivated as they get closer to a goal (goal gradient). Unpredictability keeps people searching. People are motivated by progress, mastery, and autonomy. People are inherently lazy.
- **Design implication:** Show progress visually (progress bars work). Use variable rewards (not predictable ones). Give users control.

### 7. People Are Social Animals (Things 63-71)
The brain is wired for social connection. Dunbar's number: 150. Laughter, empathy, and mirroring are hardwired.
- **Key principles:** The "strong tie" group size limit is 150. Doing things together bonds people. People expect online interactions to follow social rules. Brains sync up during communication.
- **Design implication:** Design for social connection. Make sharing easy. Use real human faces, not stock photos.

### 8. How People Feel (Things 72-84)
Emotion drives decisions. If people can't feel, they can't choose.
- **Key principles:** Some emotions are universal across cultures. Stories persuade more than data. Pastoral scenes make people happy. The more difficult to achieve, the more people like it. People want familiarity when sad or scared.
- **Design implication:** Make users feel something. Use stories, not statistics. Familiar patterns are comforting in stressful situations.

> **Case: The emotional decision** (Thing 75): Neuroscientist Antonio Damasio's patients with damage to the emotional centers of the brain could describe logical pros and cons perfectly — but they could not make decisions. They'd spend hours deliberating over trivial choices. **Key takeaway:** Emotion is not opposed to logic — it's the engine of decision. If your design doesn't make people feel anything, they won't act.

### 9. How People Make Mistakes (Things 85-89)
Errors are inevitable. Predictable error types can be designed for.
- **Key principles:** There is no fail-safe product. People make errors under stress. Errors are predictable (slips, mistakes). People use different error strategies.
- **Design implication:** Design for error recovery, not error prevention alone. Test under stress conditions.

### 10. How People Decide (Things 90-100)
Most decisions are unconscious. People want more choices than they can process.
- **Key principles:** The unconscious knows first. Choice ≠ better outcomes. People think choice equals control. Mood influences decisions. People are influenced by physical proximity. Social proof is powerful when people are uncertain.
- **Design implication:** Limit choices to 4. Design for the unconscious first. Use social proof. Make the default the desired action.
