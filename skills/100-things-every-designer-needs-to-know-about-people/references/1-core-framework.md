# Core Framework: Perception, Memory & Decision Architecture

> Source: "100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People" by Susan M. Weinschenk, Ph.D. (Second Edition, 2020) — Chapters 1-8 (How People See), 19-26 (How People Remember), 90-100 (How People Decide)

## 1. Vision Constructs Reality, It Doesn't Record It

The book's most foundational insight: what people "see" is not what their eyes transmit. The brain actively constructs visual experience using shortcuts, expectations, and patterns.

### Key Mechanisms

| Mechanism | What It Means for Design |
|---|---|
| **Kanizsa Illusion** (Ch. 1) | The brain fills in missing information to create expected shapes. Use implied shapes and negative space — people will complete them |
| **Peripheral Gist Extraction** (Ch. 2) | Peripheral vision determines scene category before central vision resolves detail. The periphery tells people "what kind of page this is" |
| **Geon Theory** (Ch. 3) | People recognize objects via 24 basic geometric shapes (geons). Simple icons = faster recognition. Complex embellishments = slower processing |
| **Fusiform Face Area (FFA)** (Ch. 4) | A dedicated brain region processes faces instantly and emotionally. Faces bypass normal visual interpretation channels |

> **Case: The Gorilla Video** (Ch. 8): In the famous selective-attention test, 50% of people watching a basketball video fail to notice a person in a gorilla suit walking through the scene, even though eye tracking shows their central gaze landed on it. **Key takeaway**: Looking ≠ seeing. Eye-tracking data tells you where eyes land, not what the brain processes.

> **Case: Müller-Lyer Illusion** (Ch. 1): Two lines of identical length appear different based on arrowhead orientation. **Key takeaway**: Context changes perception. What surrounds an element changes how it's perceived.

## 2. Memory Architecture: Working, Long-Term, and the 4±1 Rule

The book dismantles the urban legend of "7±2" (George Miller's 1956 talk) and replaces it with the research-backed **4±1 limit** (Cowan, Baddeley).

### Memory Types

| Type | Capacity | Duration | Design Implications |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Working Memory** | 3-4 items | ~20 seconds without rehearsal | Never ask users to hold info across screens. Use recognition not recall |
| **Long-Term Memory** | Vast, schema-based | Years | New info sticks when connected to existing schemas. Repetition changes brain wiring |
| **Flashbulb Memory** | Vivid but inaccurate | Lifetime | Emotionally charged memories feel true but are often wrong — don't trust user "vivid recall" in research |

> **Case: The Challenger Study** (Ch. 26): Neisser asked students to write their memory of the Challenger explosion the day after. Three years later, 90% of accounts differed, 50% were inaccurate in 2/3 of details. **Key takeaway**: Even vivid, emotionally charged memories are reconstructed, not archived.

### The Forgetting Curve (Ebbinghaus, Ch. 25)
People forget information rapidly unless it is stored in long-term memory through repetition or schema connection. Design with forgetting in mind — provide information, don't expect users to remember it.

## 3. Decision-Making: Unconscious First

### The Two-System Model (Ch. 90-100)

Weinschenk synthesizes research showing that most decisions are made unconsciously before the conscious mind catches up:

- **Unconscious knows first** (Ch. 91): The brain processes and decides before conscious awareness
- **Choice = Control illusion** (Ch. 93): People believe more choices give them more control, but excessive choice degrades decision quality
- **Mood drives decisions** (Ch. 95): Positive mood → more intuitive decisions. Negative mood → more analytical, slower decisions
- **Habit vs. Value switching** (Ch. 97): People make decisions one way (habit = automatic) or another (value = deliberate), but never both simultaneously
- **Uncertainty → Social proof** (Ch. 98): When uncertain, people let others decide — testimonials, ratings, and social signals are powerful

> **Case: Loftus Car Crash Study** (Ch. 24): Changing one word from "hit" to "smashed" doubled the number of participants who "remembered" broken glass that didn't exist. **Key takeaway**: Word choice in research and UX copy literally changes what users remember.

## 4. Load Hierarchy: Cognitive > Visual > Motor

The mental resource framework (Ch. 28):
- **Cognitive load** — thinking, remembering, calculating (most expensive)
- **Visual load** — finding, scanning, searching (moderate)
- **Motor load** — clicking, typing, moving (least expensive)

**Trade-off rule**: When in doubt, add clicks (motor) to reduce thinking (cognitive). People don't mind clicking if they don't have to think.
