## Author Voice & Personality

### Susan Weinschenk's Voice
- **Behavioral scientist meets practical designer:** Weinschenk holds a Ph.D. in psychology and has spent 30+ years applying behavioral science to design. Her writing bridges the gap between lab research and product reality.
- **Evidence-driven, not opinion-based:** Every principle is backed by cited studies from neuroscience, cognitive psychology, or behavioral economics. The book is structured as a series of "things" — each thing is a verifiable truth about human behavior.
- **Actionable, not academic:** Each principle includes immediate design implications. She doesn't just tell you how people behave — she tells you what to do about it.
- **Key phrase:** "What people see is not what their brain gets."

## 5 Common Design Scenarios

### Scenario 1: "Users Are Bouncing from My Landing Page"
**High traffic, low conversion. Users leave within seconds.**
- **What to do:** 1) Apply the F-pattern: put your key message and CTA in the top-left F-pattern path. 2) Headline must communicate value within 3 seconds. 3) Use a single, clear CTA — not multiple competing ones. 4) Social proof (testimonials, user count) in the scan path. 5) Test with emotional triggers (stories, faces).

### Scenario 2: "Users Abandon My Form"
**Long form, high dropout rate.**
- **What to do:** 1) Chunk into 4-field max per step. 2) Show progress indicator. 3) Use recognition (dropdown, autocomplete) not recall (fill-in-the-blank). 4) Provide forgiveness (undo, save drafts). 5) Clear error messages, not "Error 500."

### Scenario 3: "My Product Isn't Getting Used"
**Users sign up but don't come back.**
- **What to do:** 1) Create a habit loop: trigger → action → variable reward → investment. 2) Show progress toward a goal (goal gradient effect). 3) Intrinsic motivation: give users autonomy, mastery, purpose. 4) Make the first experience delightful — first impression shapes mental model.

### Scenario 4: "My Users Keep Making the Same Error"
**Predictable mistake pattern in your interface.**
- **What to do:** 1) Assume it's not user error — it's design error. 2) Test under stress conditions. 3) Provide clear, immediate feedback. 4) Make the undo path obvious. 5) Use constraints (disable impossible options) rather than warnings.

### Scenario 5: "Users Can't Navigate My App"
**People get lost, can't find features, use only a fraction of functionality.**
- **What to do:** 1) Match mental models — your navigation should behave like other apps they know. 2) Recognition over recall: show navigation options, don't make users remember. 3) 4-item chunks in navigation. 4) Search as primary navigation (many users prefer search to browsing). 5) Surface recent items.

> **Case: The decoy effect in pricing** (Thing 100): The Economist famously offered three subscription tiers: $59 for web-only, $125 for print-only, $125 for print+web. The middle tier (print-only at $125) served as a decoy — it made the print+web tier look like incredible value. Most people chose print+web. Remove the decoy, and most chose web-only. **Key takeaway:** The presence of a decoy option changes how people evaluate your offer. Design the comparison set, not just the individual prices.

> **Case: Social proof in online shopping** (Thing 99): A study showed that displaying "45 people are viewing this item right now" increased conversions by 15%. When people are uncertain, they look to others. Social proof reduces uncertainty and accelerates decisions. **Key takeaway:** Show social proof near the call to action. Make the most popular option the default choice.
