# Voice & Application: Susan Weinschenk's Style and Practical Scenarios

> Source: "100 Things Every Designer Needs to Know About People" by Susan M. Weinschenk, Ph.D. (Second Edition, 2020) — Chapters 63-84 (Social Animals, How People Feel), plus author's overall approach

## Author's Voice & Style

### Susan Weinschenk's Communication DNA

Susan Weinschenk (also known as "The Brain Lady") writes with a distinctive voice that blends:

1. **Research-rooted authority**: Every claim is anchored to a specific study with named researchers, dates, and methodology. She doesn't say "research shows" — she says "Loftus (1974) showed..." This builds credibility through specificity.

2. **Accessible translation**: Dense academic findings are translated into plain English with concrete examples. A 40-page neuroscience paper becomes a 2-page "Takeaways" section with clear do/don't guidance.

3. **Generous self-disclosure**: She shares her own biases, mistakes, and favorite tools ("I tested my blog article with readabilityformulas.com..."). This makes her approachable despite her PhD.

4. **Skeptical of received wisdom**: She actively debunks urban legends (7±2 memory, all-caps reading difficulty, serif vs. sans serif) with updated research.

5. **Structured summaries**: Every chapter ends with bulleted "Takeaways" — actionable, numbered, directly useful for designers.

### Signature Quotes

> "What your brain comes up with isn't exactly what your eyes are seeing. Your brain is constantly interpreting everything you see."

> "People are active readers. What they understand and remember from what they read depends on their previous experience."

> "Memories are actually reconstructed every time we think of them. They're not movie clips stored in the brain."

> "Reading a screen is different from reading on paper. The image is not stable — it is being refreshed, and the screen is emitting light."

> "People will always make mistakes. There is no fail-safe product."

## Practical Application Scenarios

### Scenario 1: Redesigning a Complicated Dashboard
**User says**: "My users are overwhelmed by the analytics dashboard. They can't find what they need."

**Solution using this book**:
1. Apply **progressive disclosure** (Ch. 27): Show only top 4 KPIs by default, with expandable sections for detail
2. Use the **30% rule** (Ch. 6): Place the most important metric ~30% from top-left where people naturally look for meaningful content
3. **Chunk into 4±1** (Ch. 20): Group 20 metrics into 4 categories (Performance, Engagement, Revenue, Health)
4. **Add visual affordances** (Ch. 7): Ensure clickable elements look clickable — shadows, contrast, clear hit targets
5. **Test through observation** (Ch. 24): Don't ask users what they think; watch them try to find a specific metric

### Scenario 2: Improving Signup Conversion
**User says**: "People start my signup flow but never finish."

**Solution using this book**:
1. **Reduce working memory load** (Ch. 19-20): Don't make users enter info on one page and remember it on the next
2. **Apply the goal-gradient effect** (Ch. 50): Show progress ("Step 2 of 4") — people accelerate as they approach completion
3. **Maximize autonomy** (Ch. 62): Allow users to skip optional steps; control increases motivation
4. **Error-proof** (Ch. 85-89): Validate inline, not after submission. Provide clear, specific error messages
5. **Use social proof** (Ch. 98): "Join 50,000+ users who signed up this month" reduces uncertainty

### Scenario 3: Creating Engaging Onboarding
**User says**: "Users don't complete onboarding. They skip through it or close the app."

**Solution using this book**:
1. **Break into chunks ≤10 minutes** (Ch. 44): Sustained attention maxes at 10 minutes. Split onboarding into sessions
2. **Use faces** (Ch. 4): A friendly face looking at the user creates emotional connection and directs attention
3. **Show progress visibly** (Ch. 50): Progress bars increase motivation via the goal-gradient effect
4. **Make it a story** (Ch. 33): Frame onboarding as a narrative ("You're about to discover how X helps you Y")
5. **Variable rewards** (Ch. 51-53): Surprise tips, bonus features, or unlocks at unpredictable intervals

### Scenario 4: Designing for Accessibility
**User says**: "I want to make sure my design works for people with visual impairments."

**Solution using this book**:
1. **Redundant color coding** (Ch. 11): Never use color alone to convey meaning — add icons, patterns, or text labels
2. **Test with vischeck.com** (Ch. 11): Check how your design appears to red-green and blue-yellow color deficiencies
3. **Avoid chromostereopsis** (Ch. 10): Don't put red on blue or red on green
4. **Large x-height fonts** (Ch. 16): Use fonts with large x-heights (Verdana, Tahoma) for better readability
5. **High contrast** (Ch. 17): Black text on white background is the most readable combination
6. **Generous hit targets** (Ch. 28): Apply Fitts's law — buttons and interactive elements need to be large enough to acquire easily

### Scenario 5: Running Better User Research
**User says**: "I'm planning user interviews for my new product. Help me avoid common mistakes."

**Solution using this book**:
1. **Watch, don't ask** (Ch. 24): Observe what users do rather than relying on self-reported past behavior
2. **Watch your words** (Ch. 24): The specific words in your questions can change what users "remember" or report
3. **Don't trust vivid memories** (Ch. 26): Flashbulb memories (e.g., "I remember exactly where I was when I first used your app") are vivid but often inaccurate
4. **Mixed signals** (Ch. 18): People may say they prefer option A while their behavior shows option B is better. Both data points matter
5. **Recognize the stress effect** (Ch. 86): Users under stress (like a usability test) perform worse, so build in comfort and breaks
