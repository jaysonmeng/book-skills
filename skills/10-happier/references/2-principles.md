## Key Principles

### 1. Meditation is not about feeling a certain way. It's about feeling the way you feel.

Dan quotes Jon Kabat-Zinn: the goal is not to achieve relaxation or bliss. The goal is awareness of whatever is happening right now — even if what's happening is restlessness, boredom, or panic.

> **Case: Dan's early meditation struggles** (Chapter 7): On his 10-day retreat, Dan spent the first days in a state of "buzzy, crackly, background radiation of annoyance." He wanted to feel peaceful and instead felt agitated. His teachers kept telling him: "Don't hold yourself responsible for what's happening in your head." When he finally stopped trying to achieve a special state, the practice clicked.

### 2. The comeback is the workout

The defining myth about meditation is that you need to "clear your mind." Wrong. Thoughts are not the obstacle — they're the opposing team. The entire point of meditation is to notice when you've been distracted and bring your attention back. *That* is the rep.

Dan frames it: "Relationship between thinking and meditating: thoughts are simultaneously the biggest obstacle and an unavoidable part of it — like the opposing team in basketball."

### 3. Create space between impulse and action

Mindfulness slows down the automatic reaction cycle. When someone cuts you off in traffic, the normal sequence is: stimulus → automatic reaction ("I'm pissed") → you become pissed. Mindfulness inserts a gap:

> Stimulus → awareness of feeling pissed → pause → *choice* of how to respond

This is the single most practical benefit of the practice.

### 4. Secular is fine. Better, even.

Dan was a lifelong atheist before discovering meditation. His entire approach is built for people who would never step foot in a yoga studio. Key principle: strip away the "sacred spaces," "divine mother," and "holding your emotions with love and tenderness" language. The science stands on its own.

### 5. Edge and mindfulness are compatible

Dan's biggest fear going in: "If I quiet the voice in my head, will I lose my edge?" His answer after years of testing: meditation doesn't kill ambition — it cleans up the noise around it. You still want to succeed; you just don't torture yourself in the process.

> **Case: Dan's career after meditation** (Chapter 9-11): After starting a regular practice, Dan continued to co-anchor Nightline and GMA, report from war zones, and compete in one of the most cutthroat industries in the world. He didn't lose his edge. He gained better focus and fewer tantrums.

### 6. Regularity > duration

Five minutes a day beats thirty minutes once a week. The habit of daily sitting is what builds the mindfulness muscle, not marathon sessions. Dan set a timer and sat wherever he could fit it in — hotel rooms, airport lounges, his office.

### 7. You can't control what comes up, only how you respond

Your first thought when you sit down to meditate might be: "This is stupid. I have better things to do." That's not *you* summoning that complaint — it comes out of the void. Don't get into a "judgment jag" about it. Just note it ("doubting," "resisting") and return to the breath. This principle applies Off the cushion: just because your kids are driving you nuts doesn't mean you're a bad person.
